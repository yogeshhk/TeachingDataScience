\begin{frame}[fragile]\frametitle{What Is a Small Model?}
\begin{itemize}
\item Here: roughly 0.5 to 4 billion parameters (the numbers a model learns), small enough for a laptop CPU.
\item Models used in the examples:
	\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Qwen3 1.7B}: Apache-2.0 licence (open source).
	\item \textbf{Gemma 2 2B}: Google's own terms of use; about 2.6 billion parameters.
	\end{itemize}
\item Small does not mean toy: pick the job first, then the smallest model that does it well enough, and measure on your own task.
\item Next: ways models are made small or cheap.
\end{itemize}
{\tiny (Ref: Hugging Face model cards for Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B and google/gemma-2-2b-it)}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}[fragile]\frametitle{Quantisation: Fewer Bits per Weight}
\begin{center}
\adjustbox{max width=0.6\linewidth, max totalheight=0.30\textheight}{\includegraphics{frugal_gemma_sizes}}
\end{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
\item Quantisation stores each weight (a number in the model) in fewer bits. Gemma 2 2B: 10.5 GB full, 1.71 GB at 4 bits (Q4\_K\_M).
\item \textbf{Q4}: about 4 bits per weight. \textbf{K}: K-quant scheme (weights in blocks). \textbf{S, M, L}: small, medium, large mix (sensitive layers keep more bits). \textbf{IQ}: newer 2 to 4 bit family; \textbf{XS, XXS}: its smallest tiers.
\item \textbf{Rule of thumb}: fewer bits, smaller file, usually faster, with some quality loss. Dhir 2B on one laptop CPU: 21 tokens per second at Q4\_K\_M, 16 at Q8\_0. Compare the same model only.
\end{itemize}
{\tiny (Ref: Hugging Face, lmstudio-community/gemma-2-2b-it-GGUF, file sizes; llama.cpp quantize tool; our run, 14 November 2026, Intel i7-1255U)}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}[fragile]\frametitle{A 2B Model Against a Microcontroller}
\begin{center}
\adjustbox{max width=0.82\linewidth, max totalheight=0.45\textheight}{\includegraphics{frugal_size_vs_memory}}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
\item The 1.71 GB file is about 6,700 times the 256 KB of RAM (working memory) on an Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense. A language model does not fit there; small classifiers do.
\item A Raspberry Pi 3 has 1 GB of RAM, so this file does not fit there either; a smaller model is needed. An 8 GB laptop is enough.
\end{itemize}
{\tiny (Ref: Nordic nRF52840; Raspberry Pi 3 Model B page, 1 GB to be checked on our board; AIOOLL README; decimal units)}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]\frametitle{Distillation: Teacher and Student}
\begin{center}
\adjustbox{max width=0.85\linewidth, max totalheight=0.26\textheight}{%
\begin{tikzpicture}
\tikzstyle{b}=[draw, rounded corners, align=center, minimum height=1.1cm, minimum width=2.8cm, font=\footnotesize]
\node[b, fill=blue!10] (t) at (0,0) {\textbf{Teacher}\\large model};
\node[b, fill=orange!15] (s) at (6.2,0) {\textbf{Student}\\small model};
\draw[->, thick] (t) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize] {its answers guide} (s);
\node[font=\scriptsize] at (3.1,-0.9) {the student learns to imitate the teacher};
\end{tikzpicture}}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
\item A small \textbf{student} model learns to imitate a large \textbf{teacher}, keeping most of the skill in far fewer parameters.
\item Example: MoScNet reads Modi script (an old script for Marathi). Its authors report a student 163 times smaller than its teacher that outperforms it, on their data.
\item The large model's work is reused, not repeated.
\end{itemize}
{\tiny (Ref: Kausadikar et al., arXiv 2503.13060)}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}[fragile]\frametitle{Memory: The KV Cache}
\begin{itemize}
\item The model keeps notes on every token it has read (the KV cache, short for keys and values) so it need not recompute them.
\item A serving paper: this memory per request ``is huge and grows and shrinks dynamically''.
\item So long prompts cost memory as well as time: a lean prompt is a frugal practice.
\end{itemize}
{\tiny (Ref: Kwon et al., PagedAttention, arXiv 2309.06180)}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}[fragile]\frametitle{Mixture of Experts}
\begin{itemize}
\item A router picks a few expert blocks per token instead of the whole network.
\item Mixtral: 8 experts per layer, 2 used per token; 47 billion parameters in total, 13 billion active per token.
\item The catch: all the weights still sit in memory. It saves compute more than memory, so it is not automatically a fit for small devices.
\end{itemize}
{\tiny (Ref: Jiang et al., Mixtral of Experts, arXiv 2401.04088)}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]\frametitle{Frozen Encoder Plus a Small Head}
\begin{center}
\adjustbox{max width=0.9\linewidth, max totalheight=0.22\textheight}{%
\begin{tikzpicture}
\tikzstyle{b}=[draw, rounded corners, align=center, minimum height=1cm, font=\footnotesize]
\node[b] (x) at (0,0) {input\\(image, audio)};
\node[b, fill=blue!12, minimum width=3.2cm] (e) at (3.8,0) {\textbf{Encoder}\\large, frozen};
\node[b, fill=orange!18, minimum width=2.6cm] (h) at (8,0) {\textbf{Small head}\\trained on your data};
\node[b] (y) at (11.3,0) {label};
\draw[->, thick] (x) -- (e); \draw[->, thick] (e) -- (h); \draw[->, thick] (h) -- (y);
\end{tikzpicture}}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
\item Train a large encoder once, keep it frozen, and train only a small head on top for your task.
\item Same idea as LoRA in the fine-tuning project: freeze the big part, train a small added part.
% [commented 2026-11-16 at the author's request: V-JEPA 2 and LeJEPA are not widely known]
% \item V-JEPA 2's paper evaluates this way, with a small attention-based probe on a frozen encoder; LeJEPA reports 79\% on ImageNet-1k with a frozen backbone and a linear probe (the authors' claim).
\item Why it is frugal: only the small head needs your data and compute.
\item The catch: the encoder still runs at prediction time, so it is not free on small hardware.
\end{itemize}
% {\tiny (Ref: Assran et al., arXiv 2506.09985; Balestriero and LeCun, arXiv 2511.08544)}
\end{frame}
